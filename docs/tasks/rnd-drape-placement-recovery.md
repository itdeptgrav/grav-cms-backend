## Reference workflow, not a claim about CLO's proprietary solver

CLO's public documentation places pattern pieces on avatar bounding-volume arrangement points, assigning body pieces to the body, sleeves to the arms, and bands/collars to the neck. Arrangement can be adjusted before sewing and simulation. CLO also documents temporary tacks to hold cloth to an avatar during simulation. Use those *workflow concepts*; do not claim our numerical solver is CLO's or infer undocumented implementation details.

### Source-fold metric check, 4 October

On the pattern-derived mesh seeded from the paired CLO UV surface, **94 of 4,782 flat-rest stretch edges (1.97%)** are more than 25% shorter in the reference 3D pose; **none** are more than 25% longer. By role: front 23, back 20, sleeves 48, neck band 3. Only 11 touch a nearest-boundary UV fallback. The most compressed left-sleeve edge spans **19.8 mm in the flat pattern but only 0.66 mm as a 3D chord**. Each endpoint is contained by exactly one source UV triangle; those are different triangles (56 and 1), so the discrepancy is not explained by choosing one of several overlapping UV triangles. It may represent a tight CLO fold, or a surface-correspondence problem between triangles. Do not label it a fabric stretch or delete the pattern edge without distinguishing these cases.

The original GLB fabric triangles are not generally degenerate. Source-primitive edge-length ratio quantiles (3D length / UV length, ignoring the duplicated front/back/edge primitives) have medians **0.972 front, 0.977 back, 0.985/0.987 sleeves, 0.943 neck band**. Minimum source-triangle edge ratios are **0.654, 0.865, 0.529, 0.697, 0.561** in that order. Thus the near-zero *chord* between two remeshed vertices comes from their 3D positions across triangles, not an equally collapsed edge within one source triangle. The rib band's substantial compression is real in the supplied reference and must not be treated as the same material as the main knit.

The worst left-sleeve case is now disambiguated: its two points lie in **different source triangles, 56 and 1**, which share source vertex 110. Their direct 3D chord is **0.66 mm**, but a surface path through that shared vertex is **21.63 mm**, close to their **19.80 mm flat-UV separation**. The source triangle normals differ by **172.6°**. This is a real, sharply folded surface—not a 20 mm fabric edge physically compressed to 0.66 mm and not an ambiguous overlapping-UV lookup. The coarse remesh connects across the fold and loses the surface path. Treating that shortcut as a stretch edge is the concrete calibration error to fix.

Splitting that exact 2D segment where it crosses the original source triangles gives four subsegments, with 3D/flat length ratios **0.981, 1.021, 0.932, 0.805** and an **18.26 mm** total surface-polyline length. The same endpoints as one unsplit edge gave a false ratio of **0.033**. Uniform subdivision does not guarantee the fix: at 16 equal parts, one subsegment still crosses the crease and has a ratio of **0.090**. This is evidence for *crease-aligned* local topology, not merely “use High quality everywhere.” The source GLB can identify this calibration case; general conversion must detect or resolve folds during simulation without treating uploaded 3D positions as the canonical pattern.

As an **isolated diagnostic only**, suppressing the 94 outlier stretch constraints while keeping the other flat-pattern rest lengths and disabling body collision let a reference-seeded Draft run retain a recognisable, smoother shirt; final seam gap was **0.18 mm mean / 1.33 mm worst**. This is not an acceptable implementation: it depends on the uploaded GLB for placement, omits body contact, and silently removing real cut constraints would undermine measurement fidelity. It does isolate a small set of fold/correspondence conflicts as a tractable next target. Inspect the 94 edges' local source-surface paths and fold angles, then represent compressed fabric with bending/contact rather than either forcing every 3D chord to equal its 2D cut length or throwing out the 2D edges.

Increasing the pattern-derived mesh to Normal spacing changes this same source-seeded edge check from **94/4,782 (1.97%)** over-25%-compressed edges at Draft to **153/28,345 (0.54%)** at Normal. Mean absolute edge strain falls **4.62% → 3.42%** and the worst compression becomes **82.7% rather than 96.7%**. Refinement therefore reduces the *fraction* and severity of these cross-fold chords but does not eliminate them; it is not evidence that the Normal drape passes, since its independent 2D-only result remains visibly collapsed. The likely engineering requirement is local fold-aware refinement or a compression/bending/contact model, not a global quality increase by itself.

At High spacing, this source-seeded check still has **265/53,424 (0.50%)** over-25%-compressed edges, **3.32% mean absolute strain**, and **90.6% worst compression**. A uniformly finer mesh remains insufficient and is computationally expensive; the fold needs a correctly represented local path and contact, not more global subdivisions alone.

### Fold-guide prototype: numeric gain, visual rejection

An isolated prototype inserted interior mesh vertices sampled along sharp fold edges from the paired GLB. This was a **calibration experiment**, not a production dependency on the GLB. At Draft spacing, using source folds above 120° lowered the over-25%-compressed reference-seeded edge count **94 → 78**. Lowering the guide threshold to 90° across all five pieces reduced it to **63**, and 60° reduced it to **49**. However, the prototype used ordinary Delaunay triangulation, so merely inserting guide *points* did not force mesh *edges* to follow a crease; edges could still cut across it. The 60° run's front image still shows an irregular collar, deep armhole creases and angular sleeves. It is visually rejected, even though the numeric mismatch improved. The prototype changed only files under an isolated `/tmp` copy, not the app.

Do not ship a threshold or a larger set of guide points based on this result. The next mesh experiment must preserve the **crease as a constrained path**, verify that no simulation edge spans across it, and then repeat the known-good-reference and independent-2D visual gates. Because a fresh DXF will not include a CLO GLB, any eventual app algorithm needs to detect/resolve such folds from its own simulated state or use a clearly labelled optional calibration reference. A paired GLB is an oracle for this case, not permission to make 3D the source of truth.

The next isolated experiment clarified the limit. Adding explicit fold-edge constraints to this point-guided triangulation made **zero edge flips**: Delaunay had already connected the retained guide vertices along every attempted interior crease segment. At the 60° threshold, **407 of 442 requested subsegments** were already edges; the others lacked an exact endpoint after guide filtering near the boundary. The severe reference-seeded edge count stayed **48**, exactly the same as with pinned guide points but without explicit constraints. Therefore the rejected visual result is **not** explained simply by the absence of those constrained edges. The earlier 19.8 mm shortcut is real, but it is one local failure, not proof that fixing crease topology alone will make the garment credible.

A separate adaptive edge-splitting prototype was also rejected: splitting every currently severe edge for four rounds created new crossing diagonals and increased the severe count **94 → 314**; the rendered collar and armholes remained visibly defective. It did not modify app code. Do not implement naive midpoint/crease-point subdivision as a fix. The remaining work requires the complete fabric/contact/seam system to hold a folded cloth surface, and the paired DXF/GLB alone omit the CLO material parameters and avatar that produced that surface.

## Acceptance gate

- Every source pattern edge is preserved; no hidden geometry replacement or 2D write-back.
- No vertex starts or finishes inside the avatar; no detached panels, inverted panels, or self-intersection visible in any view.
- Seams are closed without extreme local stretch (target: each seam mean at most 6 mm, mean edge strain at most 2%, maximum edge strain at most 25%; report maxima as well as averages).
- Neck band reads as a continuous band; sleeves read as two attached open tubes rather than wedges or accordion folds; shoulders, torso and hem look like a plausible T-shirt in all five views.
- Simulation settles, is repeatable, and the app labels a nonpassing result **preview / not fit-accurate**.
- The real tee is visually reviewed before a change is committed or described as successful. Numeric tests alone cannot pass this gate.

Until this gate passes, the answer to “is the T-shirt 2D-to-3D feature done?” remains **no**. Other garment categories are outside this slice.

## Open-source comparison gate, 5 October 2026

This was an isolated trial, not a change to the R&D app or its engine decision. Temporary checkouts and renders are under `/tmp/garment-oss.1dZ8fc/`. The uploaded DXF, CLO project, XML, GLB and avatar were not edited.

- **OpenSew-2 / Blender 5.2.2:** Its own preset tests, including sewing and draping its *built-in* T-shirt on both bundled avatars, reported zero failures. The displayed-vertex penetration counts were 28/8,302 (woman) and 43/11,574 (man). Independent front/back/left renders (`opensew-*.png`) are **visually rejected**: the shoulder/armhole region appears torn or folded, the neck is loose, and the hem rides high. Passing a penetration test is not a visual acceptance gate. Its preset has four pieces and the add-on does not directly import the supplied five-piece AAMA DXF. Its GPL-3.0-or-later license needs separate distribution review before any code reuse; the stitch→weld→settle idea can be evaluated independently without copying implementation.
- **Blender Design Studio:** Its actual DXF importer was run on `/Users/risheeray/Downloads/tshirt.dxf`. It returned `FINISHED` but created one object with **10 vertices, 5 edges, zero faces**. This is not the five pattern pieces. The implementation handles `LINE` and `LWPOLYLINE`, while this AAMA file carries its cut outlines as legacy `POLYLINE` entities in `BLOCK`s. The README's generic DXF support does not establish compatibility with this file.
- **Seamer Studio / Atelier:** Its DXF parser read this exact file as **27 polylines, five closed outlines**, with five `PIECE NAME` labels. Its full unit suite passed **1,054/1,054** after the declared sibling engine and body-model submodule were installed. But the parser defaults to millimetres without `$INSUNITS`, whereas the source file says `UNITS: ENGLISH`; an explicit AAMA unit conversion is required. More importantly, the project's own checkpoint reports **0/7 garments** passing its full released-drape acceptance test. Parsing all five outlines is useful, but is not evidence of a believable drape.
- **GarmentCode + its NvidiaWarp fork:** The core pattern library is MIT, but its documented simulation path requires the forked Warp runtime and a CUDA build; this Mac has no CUDA toolchain. The fork's `LICENSE.md` defines non-commercial use as *research or evaluation purposes only*. Internal use at a clothing business is not automatically cleared for an operational feature. No simulation was run or integrated under that license.

**Decision from the trial:** no candidate is a proven drop-in replacement for the exact five-piece CLO T-shirt. Do not move the app to Blender, Seamer or Warp on the strength of these tests. The next independent proof must consume the DXF's five measured outlines, the XML's **15** source seam pairs (including the neckband's self-join/fold), verified units, the correct avatar and piece-specific fabric records. Compare generated front/back/both sides and armhole close-ups against the GLB oracle. The original GLB remains comparison evidence only, never a generated result. Reject numerically passing but visually distorted output.

### Real-file stitch→weld ablation

A further scratch-only Blender test took the actual five-piece DXF-derived cloth after the existing 60-frame, 50 N sewing pass, welded its seam-paired vertices into shared topology, disabled the continuing sewing pulls, and settled for 60 more frames with the actual FBX avatar collider. This is a **diagnostic, not a measurement-valid candidate**: welding bakes the already deformed 3D pose as a new cloth rest shape. Before welding, 29 cut-mesh edges were below 70% of their 2D rest lengths (worst 46%). The resulting front/back/side renders are `/tmp/garment-oss.1dZ8fc/real-dxf-weld-*.png`. They are visually rejected: jagged/open neckline, crumpled sleeves, long torso chevrons, and a torn-looking armhole from the side. The test shows that removing permanent seam-pull pressure after late welding does not undo distortion created during the initial assembly. It does **not** disprove a different, pattern-preserving weld strategy that starts from a better arranged garment. No source file or app code changed.

### Exact-source calibration check, 5 October

The 15 CLO stitch pairs are not 15 independent joins missing from the app's 10 groups: the app combines each two-part sleeve cap into one armhole group and three band-to-neckline pairs into one group. The CLO band long-edge fold (pair 15) is represented in the app by a derived **half-width, two-layer** strip, not by a second explicit stitch. The cut-edge paths of the other grouped joins match the source within about 0–4 mm in the prior 2D path comparison. The count difference alone is therefore not the cause.

One true correspondence difference was measured at the neck: CLO band stations 0–4.3% meet neckline stations 95.8–100%, then band 4.3–57.2% meets neckline 0–54.2%. This is a cyclic phase offset at the band join, whereas the app's combined seam uses uniform start-to-start arc fractions. In a scratch-only run I changed the neck matching to the CLO station mapping, while retaining the DXF cut lengths, the existing armhole station correction and actual FBX avatar contact. The independent Draft run still returned **`partial`** in 96 seconds. Its sleeve openings collapsed to **13% and 15% roundness**, differed by **61%**, and 48 cut-mesh edges were compressed beyond the geometry warning threshold. The front/back/side images at `/tmp/clo-tshirt.974guT/source-neck-avatar-body-*.png` show crumpled sleeve openings and a broken-looking neckline, so this candidate is **visually rejected**. The neck phase mismatch is real, but fixing it alone does not solve the garment.

The source material records also do not match the current single app profile. The supplied CLO XML names **Knit_Fleece_Terry_0** at approximately **226 gsm** and **Rib_1X1_319gsm_0** at approximately **319 gsm**, both with a recorded friction coefficient of **0.03**. The GLB confirms the actual assignment: front, back, and both sleeves use `Knit_Fleece_Terry_FRONT_2770`, whereas `Pattern_2091816` (the band) uses `Rib_1X1_319gsm_FRONT_2759`. The app fixture assigns all five pieces one **180 gsm** jersey profile with body friction **0.5**; its rib band has no separate fabric profile. CLO stiffness values and the app's percentages are not dimensionally interchangeable, so copying the raw numbers would be false calibration. Piece-specific material assignment and a measured conversion contract are needed before claiming source-faithful physics. A previous isolated 0.03 body-friction test improved some pleat findings but did not pass the side-view sleeve gate.

For another quantitative reference, unique triangle edges of the actual CLO GLB were compared in 3D against its exported UV pattern lengths (ignoring edges shorter than 0.5 mm or longer than 40 mm). Front/back medians are **0.973/0.975**, with 5th percentiles **0.919/0.933**; sleeve medians are **0.982/0.984**. Mean absolute deviations from flat are **3.24% front, 3.04% back, 3.74% left sleeve, 3.93% right sleeve and 6.42% band**. The folded neck band is much more compressed locally (5th percentile **0.825**, 230 of 15,526 sampled edges below 0.75). These are source-export geometry ratios, not material-test readings. The existing universal **≤2% mean edge strain** target is *stricter than this actual CLO garment's exported geometry* and therefore cannot be used as proof that a render matches CLO. Replace it with a piece-aware, source-relative validation *alongside* silhouette and seam checks; do not weaken the pattern's immutable rest metric or hide a distorted derived render.

**Correction of a diagnostic error:** The first hold-test script mirrored the right sleeve backwards when mapping the CLO UVs to the pattern-derived test mesh. It began with a **91.8 mm mean right-armhole gap**, so its initial four-way results and earlier ablation numbers were invalid evidence about the solver. The corrected orientation starts both armholes at **2.19/2.95 mm mean** and the whole seam set at **4.17 mm mean**. The original erroneous logs were overwritten with corrected runs; do not cite the old numbers.

With that correction, a four-way known-good-pose hold test separated source mass from source friction, using the paired CLO pose only as a diagnostic initial condition, the pattern-derived mesh/rest lengths, the source-derived armhole and neck stations, and actual FBX avatar contact. After 60 frames, the current **180 gsm / 0.5 friction** profile had **38.1 mm mean vertex drift**, **3.97 mm mean seam gap**, **5 compressed edges** and *no automatic surface findings*. Changing only the main/rib masses to **226/319 gsm** gave **38.2 mm / 3.88 mm**, with a sleeve-opening finding. Changing only solver friction to the XML's **0.03** gave **53.9 mm / 3.74 mm**, with a collar finding; changing both gave **54.1 mm / 3.86 mm**, with collar/sleeve findings. The corrected default-profile front/back/side renders at `/tmp/clo-tshirt.974guT/reference-hold-corrected-final-*.png` are much more recognisably shirt-shaped than the prior erroneous hold, but the front neckline lacks a coherent band and the side views retain dark armhole gaps. This is **not an independent 2D-to-3D drape**: it starts from the user's CLO 3D pose and is a solver diagnostic only. Mass alone does not fix the remaining defects; copying CLO's nominal friction into our different contact law makes the source-seeded hold worse. Stiffness units and constitutive meanings are still unmatched.

The corrected first-frame hold was ablated one mechanism at a time: **6.38 mm** mean displacement with all forces, **5.99 mm** without avatar contact, **6.51 mm** without seam forces, **3.98 mm** without stretch, **6.36 mm** without bend and **6.22 mm** without gravity. With seam and stretch both disabled it moved **3.58 mm** (or **2.23 mm** without avatar contact too). These are diagnostic one-frame means, not a valid simulation. The main immediate perturbation of the corrected source pose is now the **flat-rest stretch constraint**, not seam attraction; seam attraction helps hold it together. This points to rest-geometry correspondence and compression handling, while the independent flat-to-3D assembly remains unsolved. Scratch artifacts: `/tmp/clo-tshirt.974guT/reference-hold-physical*.log` and corresponding frame JSONs. No live app code changed.

A separate five-piece rigid-placement test tried to fit only one proper rotation/translation per pattern piece to the CLO reference, preserving all piece-local 2D-derived lengths. It reduced starting shoulder gaps from roughly **288/292 mm** to **76/80 mm** and the band-to-neck mean from **256 mm** to **51 mm**, but the armholes still started about **103/99 mm** apart and the sleeve-underarm/band self-joins did not change because a rigid transform cannot fold a piece. More decisively, against the actual assembly-pose FBX avatar the transformed pieces placed **69 front, 92 back, 50 left-sleeve and 55 right-sleeve vertices inside** the body (up to **40–60 mm** deep). The rigid-fit placement is rejected and was never simulated as a candidate. It demonstrates that moving whole sheets closer to the known source does not create a collision-free assembly; shape and contact must be resolved together. Scratch audit: `/tmp/clo-tshirt.974guT/rigid-placement-audit.py` and its JSON output.

A final diagnostic substituted **CLO-pose edge lengths** for the flat-pattern stretch rest lengths, test-only, to see whether that single constraint explained the known-good pose drifting. It did not. With source armhole stations and actual avatar collision, the source-seeded shape still drifted **31.6 mm mean** after 60 frames, its seams averaged **3.46 mm** but had a **20.35 mm worst** station, and **66 edges** compressed below 70% of their rest length (worst 16%). Front and side renders at `/tmp/clo-tshirt.974guT/source-rest-test-*.png` show a recognizable T-shirt silhouette but an absent/broken neck band and a visible open shoulder/armhole line. Adding exact source neck station phases worsened collar serration and sleeve symmetry, and left 67 compressed edges. Moreover, the paired CLO garment mapped onto the supplied FBX avatar starts with **42 of 1,769 sampled cloth vertices deeper than 2 mm inside the body**, deepest **14.4 mm**; this is a real alignment/contact mismatch in the diagnostic, not a reason to alter the user's originals. The source-pose-length substitution is rejected as a production fix: it changes the authoritative cut metric and still fails the visual and geometry gates. The next step must solve avatar/garment registration and the neck/armhole topology while retaining DXF cut lengths, then re-run an independent flat-to-3D visual proof.

**Reference-guided preview audit:** The paired GLB can be UV-sampled onto the DXF-derived five-piece triangulation as a diagnostic reference shape, but the result is not an independent DXF-to-3D conversion. A four-orientation search found that the neck band's vertical UV orientation had been reversed in the earlier diagnostic. Correcting that and using the source neck seam phases reduced the initial band-to-neck mean gap from **24.37 mm** to **5.59 mm** (worst **10.98 mm**); the band self-join stayed around **1.56 mm**. The piece-coloured render `/tmp/clo-tshirt.974guT/band-correct-colored-back.png` shows the neck band, and also exposed a camera-label mistake: the old scratch renderer labelled the garment's actual front as `back`. Its labels were corrected for subsequent tests, so old front/back filenames should not be cited as orientation evidence. Even before any solver frame, this source-guided DXF mesh has **66 stretch edges below 70% of the true flat cut length** (10 front, 12 back, 18 left sleeve, 24 right sleeve, 2 band; worst 16%) and **60 vertices inside the supplied avatar** by the model-health >1 mm threshold (42 deeper than 2 mm; deepest 14.4 mm). Both sleeve openings pass the coarse shape detector but these geometric defects fail approval. This is a correspondence/registration problem, not merely bad lighting. Do not ship the GLB-sampled mesh or present it as a completed 2D-to-3D feature.

The UV correspondence was challenged rather than assumed. Most pattern-derived vertices have two to four source UV triangle hits (588/647 contained front points, 593/664 back, 125/159 and 123/155 sleeves), but their candidate **3D positions differ by at most about 2 mm**—consistent with CLO's two fabric faces. Choosing the first of these hits cannot explain a 16% edge-length ratio. On the original CLO GLB's own fine topology, **zero** front/back edges and only **2/4** sleeve edges fall below 70% of their UV length; the band has 126 such edges across its fold. Increasing the DXF-derived mesh from 1,769 Draft vertices to 9,741 Normal vertices still left **78** compressed stretch edges, worst about 15%. Thus both the initial coarse and finer interpolated reference copies introduce large chord/registration errors around folds that the source topology does not. Finer uniform meshing alone is not a safe conversion strategy. A valid mapping would need source-density/adaptive topology and collision-aware avatar registration; those have not been implemented or passed. These are diagnostic tests of a GLB-guided preview, not evidence that the independent draper is ready.
